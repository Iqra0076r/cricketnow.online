Result: Hong Kong 140/5 (20 overs) beat Malaysia 72/5 (10 overs) by 7 runs (DLS method), 3rd Match, ICC Men's T20 World Cup Asia Sub Regional Qualifier B 2026, Selangor Turf Club, Kuala Lumpur.

The qualifier opened with the exact chaos that makes Associate cricket irresistible. Hong Kong posted 140 for 5, Malaysia's chase was twice interrupted — first by Ayush Shukla, then by the Kuala Lumpur rain — and when Duckworth-Lewis-Stern did the final arithmetic, Hong Kong were seven runs to the good. Fourteen days after Malaysia had thrashed them by eight wickets, Hong Kong had their revenge when it mattered.

How it was won

Hong Kong's innings was built the old-fashioned way: one substantial partnership and useful late runs. Zeeshan Ali, keeping wicket and opening, anchored the top order with 48 from 38 balls, and his 63-run stand with Reyansh Sharma (35 off 27) for the second wicket was the spine of the innings. Malaysia's spinners fought back impressively through the middle overs — Vijay Unni's 4-0-16-3 was the pick of the bowling on either side, and Virandeep Singh's 4-0-19-1 kept the scoring honest. When Zeeshan fell with the score at 105, Hong Kong could have subsided; instead Shahid Wasif's unbeaten 17 from 14 balls pushed them to 140 for 5, a total that looked competitive without looking safe.

Malaysia's reply began disastrously. Ayush Shukla struck in his first over, removing Nazmus Sakib, and by the time he had also accounted for Aqeel Wahid and Virandeep Singh, Malaysia were three down inside the powerplay and going nowhere. Shukla's 3 for 24 from three overs was the definitive new-ball spell of the day.

Then came Syed Aziz. The Malaysian batter produced the innings of the match — 40 from just 21 balls, with seven fours and a six, played at a strike rate of 190. For a brief, thrilling passage, the chase had a pulse: Aziz was finding the boundary almost at will, and Hong Kong's fielders were suddenly the ones under pressure.

But the rain had the final word. With Malaysia at 72 for 5 after 10 overs — Aziz already dismissed, Ehsan Khan having struck, and the required rate climbing — the weather closed in and no further play was possible. Under DLS, with half the chase gone and five wickets down, Malaysia were seven runs short of the par. Hong Kong took the points without bowling another ball.

Key performances

Syed Aziz (Malaysia) — 40 (21), 7×4, 1×6. The innings of the day, and ultimately a losing one. Aziz's counterattack was the only passage of play in which Malaysia looked like winning; his dismissal for 40, with the score on the slide, was the moment the chase truly died.

Ayush Shukla (Hong Kong) — 3-0-24-3. Three powerplay wickets, including two in the top order, broke Malaysia's chase before it could breathe. In a rain-shortened game, early wickets are worth double — Shukla delivered them.

Vijay Unni (Malaysia) — 4-0-16-3. The quiet excellence of the day. While Aziz took the headlines, Unni's four overs for 16 were the reason Hong Kong never got away to 160-plus. On another day, that spell wins matches.

Zeeshan Ali (Hong Kong) — 48 (38). The innings that gave Hong Kong something to defend. Five fours and a six, and the composure to bat through the middle overs when Malaysia's spinners were tightening the screws.

DLS, in plain English

Here's what happened, without the textbook. Rain stopped play with Malaysia's chase exactly halfway through — 10 of 20 overs bowled, five wickets down, 72 on the board. The Duckworth-Lewis-Stern method exists for precisely this situation: it calculates what a team "should" have scored at the interruption point, given the wickets they've lost and the overs remaining, based on the resources consumed relative to the target.

Malaysia, five down and well behind the required rate when the rain came, were adjudged seven runs short of par. The margin — "won by 7 runs (DLS method)" — reflects the gap between their 72 and where DLS said they needed to be. It is a cold arithmetic, but a fair one: Hong Kong had banked their full twenty overs and 140 runs; Malaysia had managed neither the runs nor the resource preservation to stay ahead of the equation.

What it means

First, the immediate: Hong Kong open their campaign with a win, and it is a sweet one. When these sides last met on September 24, Malaysia rolled Hong Kong for 81 and chased it down with eight wickets and eight overs to spare. To reverse that result, in a qualifier, with rain complicating everything, is the kind of statement that builds a campaign.

Second, the arithmetic. In a short sub-regional qualifier, every win is gold and every net-run-rate decimal can decide who advances. Hong Kong will take the two points and the psychological edge; Malaysia, despite Aziz's brilliance, start with a defeat that puts immediate pressure on their remaining group games.

The race is on — and on Day 1 evidence, it will not lack for drama.
