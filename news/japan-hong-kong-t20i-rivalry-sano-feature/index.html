By CricketNow Desk · 2026-10-10

Three weeks ago, in front of a small crowd at a ground most cricket fans couldn't find on a map, Japan and Hong Kong played a T20 international that deserved a far bigger audience. Hong Kong won by four wickets, chasing 96 with five balls to spare. Outside these pages, effectively nobody wrote about it.

That is the gap CricketNow exists to fill. This is a genuine rivalry between two of Asia's most serious Associate programmes: Hong Kong, the region's established pathway side with World Cup pedigree, against a Japanese team that has built real infrastructure — its own international ground at Sano — and a playing identity to match. With both teams' seasons still unfolding, this is the story of Asian Associate cricket's most overlooked contest, told properly for once.

The rivalry: what happened at Sano

The first T20I of Hong Kong's tour of Japan, on September 19, was a proper low-scoring scrap. Japan won the toss and chose to bat on their home ground — and immediately discovered how hard Hong Kong's bowlers make you work for runs. Ehsan Khan's 3 for 18 and Yasim Murtaza's 2 for 16 kept Japan to 95 for 7 from their twenty overs, with only Benjamin Ito-Davis (35 from 34 balls) offering sustained resistance.

Ninety-six should have been a formality. It wasn't. Japan's bowlers, led by the same Ito-Davis (3 for 11 from four outstanding overs) and Charlie Hara-Hinze (2 for 21), dragged Hong Kong into the deepest of holes, and at 85 for 6 the game was genuinely in the balance. It took Shahid Wasif's unbeaten 30 from 31 balls — calm, unhurried, exactly what the situation demanded — and Ehsan Khan's 14 not out from 11 to get Hong Kong home: 99 for 6 in 19.1 overs, a four-wicket win sealed with a boundary.

The second match, scheduled for the following day, was abandoned without a ball bowled. One game, one classic, one washout — the whole emotional range of Associate cricket in 48 hours.

Form guide: what the scorecards say

Hong Kong's last five T20Is tell the story of a team finding its rhythm at exactly the right time:

  • Sep 19: beat Japan by 4 wickets (Sano)
  • Sep 20: abandoned vs Japan (Sano)
  • Sep 24: lost to Malaysia by 8 wickets (Hong Kong 81 all out)
  • Sep 25: beat Oman by 41 runs
  • Oct 8: beat Malaysia by 7 runs, DLS (Qualifier B opener, Kuala Lumpur)

The September 24 defeat to Malaysia — rolled for 81 — looks increasingly like the anomaly, not the norm. Since then: two wins from two, including the rain-affected qualifier opener where Ayush Shukla's new-ball burst and Syed Aziz's counterattack made it a match worth remembering.

Japan's recent sample is smaller, but instructive: the narrow loss to Hong Kong at Sano, the abandonment, and then a remarkable footnote — on September 22, India played their first-ever international against Japan at the same ground, a rain-shortened T20I that India won by just 2 runs. Japan pushed one of the sport's giants to the final ball on their own patch. That result says more about where Japanese cricket is heading than any ranking ever could.

Venue dossier: Sano International Cricket Ground

Sano, in Tochigi Prefecture about 100 kilometres north of Tokyo, is the headquarters of the Japan Cricket Association and the spiritual home of the sport in the country. In March 2016, the Sano City Council converted the former Tanuma High School ground into Japan's first dedicated, purpose-built cricket venue — a place that would never again have to compete with baseball or football for usage.

It is a small, intimate ground by international standards: boundaries of roughly 55 metres, capacity around 2,000. But what it lacks in scale it makes up for in character. The low, skiddy surface rewards accurate bowling — as both attacks demonstrated in September — and the ground has hosted everything from East Asia Cup fixtures to ICC pathway qualifiers. When the Asian Games needed a new cricket stadium, one was built at Nisshin near Nagoya; but Sano remains where Japanese cricket lives.

Players to watch

Shahid Wasif (Hong Kong). The unbeaten 30 at Sano was a masterclass in chase management, and his 17 not out against Malaysia steadied another wobble. Hong Kong's finisher-in-chief is in the form of his life.

Ehsan Khan (Hong Kong). Three for 18 with the ball and 14 not out with the bat in the same September game, then 2 for 7 against Malaysia. The genuine all-round article, and Hong Kong's most valuable cricketer right now.

Yasim Murtaza (Hong Kong, captain). Two for 16 at Sano and the tactical brain behind Hong Kong's revival. Leads from the front with his left-arm spin.

Benjamin Ito-Davis (Japan). The standout individual of the Sano series: 35 runs and 3 for 11 in the same match. A genuine two-skill cricketer and the face of Japan's new generation.

Charlie Hara-Hinze (Japan). Two for 21 at Sano, and the kind of lively seam bowling that troubles Associate batting line-ups. Japan's new-ball leader.

Kendel Kadowaki-Fleming (Japan). Part of the new wave of Japanese-qualified talent coming through the domestic system. The Sano series was another step in his international education.

Why it matters

Forget the rankings for a moment. What Japan are building — a dedicated home ground, a domestic league feeding the national team, fixtures against Full Members — is the actual infrastructure of cricket's global growth. Hong Kong, meanwhile, remain the standard-bearers for what a well-run Associate programme looks like: professional, competitive, and capable of beating anyone on their day, as Malaysia discovered twice in three weeks.

The pathway ahead is real. The East Asia-Pacific qualifiers for the 2028 men's T20 World Cup will be staged in Japan, with matches at both the new Nisshin venue and Sano. For Japan, that is a home World Cup qualifying campaign. For Hong Kong, every fixture against improving opposition is preparation for the Asian qualification battles that define their calendar.

Nobody previewed Japan vs Hong Kong in September. Next time these two meet — and there will be a next time — it will be harder to ignore them.

Where to watch / how to follow

Associate fixtures rarely enjoy fixed global broadcast deals. For future Japan and Hong Kong matches, check local listings and the boards' official channels around match time; ball-by-ball coverage is typically available through the major live-score platforms.
